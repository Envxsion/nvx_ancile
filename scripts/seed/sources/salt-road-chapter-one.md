# The Salt Road: Chapter One (draft 4)

The tide had already turned when Mara reached the causeway, and the posts that marked the safe way across were standing in water up to their shins. She stopped where the tarmac gave out and looked at them for a long time.

Her grandmother used to say that the island kept its own hours. You could arrive when the sea allowed and leave when the sea allowed, and everything else was impatience. Mara had been impatient for eleven years. She had left at nineteen with a rucksack and a scholarship and a promise to her mother that she would come back for Christmas, and she had not come back for Christmas, or for anything, until the letter.

The letter was in her coat pocket. She had read it so many times on the train that the folds had gone soft. It was from a solicitor in Alnmouth, and it said, in four careful paragraphs, that her grandmother had died in March, that the cottage at Ness End had been left to her, and that there was "a matter concerning the boundary of the salt pans" which the solicitor would prefer to discuss in person.

The salt pans. Mara had not thought about them in years. When she was small they were already a ruin, three square ponds behind the cottage where the sea came in through a sluice and the sun did the rest. Her grandfather had worked them until the year he drowned. After that, her grandmother let the sluice silt up and the ponds fill with reeds, and nobody on the island mentioned salt in front of her again.

A car came up behind her, slowed, and stopped. The driver wound down his window. He was about her age, with a fisherman's jumper and a face she half knew.

"You'll not get across tonight," he said. "Not on foot."

"I can see that."

"There's a room at the Ship if you need one." He looked at her more closely. "You're Agnes Wren's granddaughter."

It was not a question, and she did not answer it. Out on the water, the last of the light caught the posts one by one, and then let them go.
