# How an air-source heat pump heats a house

An air-source heat pump moves heat rather than making it. A fan draws outdoor air over a coil containing refrigerant, which evaporates even in freezing weather. A compressor raises the refrigerant's pressure and temperature, and that heat passes to the water in your radiators and hot water cylinder.

## Efficiency

Efficiency is measured as the coefficient of performance (COP): the heat delivered divided by the electricity used. A COP of 3 means three units of heat for every unit of electricity. Over a whole year this is expressed as the seasonal coefficient of performance (SCOP).

The single biggest influence on efficiency is the **flow temperature**: how hot the water leaving the heat pump is. Every degree lower improves efficiency by roughly 2 to 3%. That is why heat pump systems are designed to run at 45 to 50 °C rather than the 70 °C a gas boiler often uses, and why radiators are sometimes enlarged.

A well-designed system in a British home typically achieves a SCOP between 3.0 and 4.0.

## Running costs

At an electricity price of 24.5p per kWh and a SCOP of 3.2, each kilowatt-hour of heat costs about 7.7p. A gas boiler running at 88% efficiency with gas at 6.3p per kWh costs about 7.2p per kilowatt-hour of heat. The two are close; heat pumps pull ahead on time-of-use tariffs that are cheaper overnight and in the early afternoon.

## Comfort

Heat pumps work best left on, holding a steady temperature, rather than heating the house in two bursts a day. Rooms feel evenly warm rather than hot near the radiator. Most owners lower the setpoint by a degree after the first winter because the house no longer cools down between bursts.

## Common worries

**Noise.** Modern outdoor units produce 40 to 50 dB at one metre in normal running, similar to a quiet conversation. They are louder during defrost cycles, which last a few minutes.

**Cold weather.** Units sold in the UK are rated to keep working down to minus 15 °C or below, though efficiency falls as it gets colder.

**Hot water.** Hot water is stored in a cylinder heated to about 50 °C, with a weekly cycle to 60 °C to prevent legionella.
